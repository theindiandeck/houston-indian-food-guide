Curry: The Heart of the Menu

Curry is the word most people use for Indian gravy dishes, but it covers a wide range. Some curries are creamy and mild. Others are dark, slow cooked, and deeply spiced. At The Indian Deck, the curry menu is built to give you a full range.

Butter chicken is the most approachable curry. It is creamy, tomato based, and mild. It pairs well with naan and rice. It is a good first choice if you have never had Indian food before. Tikka masala is similar but with a little more warmth. It is creamy, but the spices are more forward.

For deeper flavour, try rogan josh. It is slow cooked, with a rich colour and a warmth that builds. Korma is milder and creamier, with a nutty base. Vindaloo is hotter and tangier, for those who like heat. Palak paneer is a vegetarian curry with spinach and paneer, creamy and comforting.

When you order curry, think about balance. If you order a creamy curry, pair it with a fresh salad or raita. If you order a hotter curry, add bread to scoop and cool the palate. If you are ordering for a group, pick one creamy, one medium, and one bold curry. That way everyone finds something they like.

Biryani: A Complete Meal in One Pot

Biryani is one of the most loved dishes at The Indian Deck. It is rice, marinated protein or vegetables, herbs, and spices, layered and cooked together so the flavours settle into the grains. Good biryani has separate grains, fragrant aroma, and a taste that changes from bite to bite.

Chicken biryani is the classic. Lamb biryani is richer and benefits from longer cooking. Vegetable biryani is not an afterthought. It is layered with the same care, so it feels complete. Each version is served in a generous portion that works for one hungry guest or two lighter eaters sharing with sides.

Biryani travels well. The layered cooking keeps the rice from drying out. It reheats well because the flavours have already settled. That is why it is one of the most requested dishes for takeout and catering. For office lunches and family gatherings, it is a reliable choice that serves a crowd without losing quality.

If this is your first biryani, start with chicken biryani, add raita, and add naan. The raita cools the palate. The naan lets you scoop the last grains. If you are vegetarian, try vegetable biryani with the same sides. The kitchen gives it the same time and layering.

Tandoor: Smoke, Char, and Fresh Bread

The tandoor is a clay oven that cooks at high heat. It gives food a smoky flavour and a charred edge that cannot be replicated on a flat grill. Tandoori chicken, seekh kebab, and paneer tikka all come from the tandoor. They are marinated, then cooked quickly so the outside chars while the inside stays juicy.

Breads also come from the tandoor. Naan is soft, slightly chewy, and perfect for scooping curry. Garlic naan adds garlic and herbs. Roti is lighter and simpler. Fresh bread is a large part of the experience. It is served hot, and it is meant to be eaten with the meal, not after it.

If you are dining with others, order a tandoori starter to share. The smoky flavour pairs well with fragrant rice and creamy curry. It also gives the table something to enjoy while the mains are prepared.

South Indian Flavours

South Indian food brings a different rhythm. Dosa is a crisp, thin crepe made from rice and lentils, served with sambar and chutney. Idli is soft, steamed, and light. These dishes are lighter than North Indian curries, and they are a good choice for lunch.

Sambar is a lentil based soup with vegetables and tamarind. It is tangy, warming, and nourishing. Chutneys add freshness. Coconut chutney is creamy and mild. Tomato chutney is tangy and bright. Mint chutney is fresh and cooling.

If you have never tried South Indian food, start with a dosa. It is fun to eat, easy to share, and a good introduction to the lighter side of Indian cooking.

Drinks That Pair Well With Indian Food

Desserts: A Sweet Finish

How Indian Food Is Cooked: Techniques

Indian cooking uses several techniques that work together. Tempering, or tadka, is the process of heating spices in oil or ghee so they release aroma. It is used at the start of many dishes and at the end as a finish. Slow cooking lets curries develop depth. The tandoor gives high heat and smoke. Dum cooking, used for biryani, seals the pot so steam does the work.

Spices are not just added. They are layered. Whole spices go in early. Ground spices go in mid cooking. Fresh herbs go in at the end. That layering is why Indian food tastes complex. Each spice has a moment. When they come together, the result is balanced.

Freshness matters. Spices lose aroma over time. Good kitchens grind and store spices carefully. Herbs are added fresh. Ginger and garlic are used fresh, not from a jar. These details add up. They are the difference between a good curry and a memorable one.
